AI in European SMEs: moving from a pilot to a reliable process

VBG Labs · 11 November 2026

The latest Eurobarometer on artificial intelligence and algorithmic management describes a familiar position: many businesses have started, but a sizeable share are still experimenting. Forty-two per cent of the SMEs and mid-caps surveyed use AI or automated systems, yet only 12% use them extensively; 30% report limited or experimental use. Moving from pilot to reliable process does not start with adding more tools. It requires a defined outcome, a clear split between AI and rules, controlled data and permissions, human oversight, and a full cost per successful case.

What the latest Eurobarometer actually says

On 14 October 2026, the European Commission published the Flash Eurobarometer 578 findings. The survey covers 12,826 telephone interviews conducted from 20 March to 13 April 2026 with privately owned businesses employing between 1 and 749 people across all 27 EU Member States.

Three qualifications matter. The sample combines SMEs and mid-caps, so it is not solely a picture of microbusinesses. The figures group artificial intelligence with algorithmic management, which may include automated systems that do not use AI. And these are EU-wide findings, not country-specific estimates for Spain or any other Member State.

With those limits in view, the findings still show where adoption is taking place:

  • 42% already use AI or automated systems: 12% extensively and 30% in a limited or experimental way;
  • 21% plan to use them in future;
  • current or planned uses concentrate on customer service and client relations (47%), performance and productivity monitoring (36%), and logistics, planning or staff scheduling (30%);
  • among users, 52% say the system makes suggestions but a person decides, while only 2% report fully automated decisions without human review.

The official Flash Eurobarometer 578 infographic identifies investment cost (50%), legal or regulatory uncertainty (49%), and a lack of internal expertise (48%) as leading barriers. These are not separate problems. A poorly scoped pilot tends to cost more, be harder to govern, and leave the business more dependent on its supplier.

The hard part is not testing AI, but turning it into a process

Opening an assistant, summarising documents or connecting a model to a form proves that the technology can perform a task. It does not prove that it can perform that task every day with real data, exceptions, permissions, failures and a named owner.

A pilot becomes a process when the team can answer these questions with evidence:

  1. what result each run leaves behind;
  2. which data it may and may not access;
  3. what the model interprets and what rules determine;
  4. when the system stops or escalates a case;
  5. who reviews exceptions;
  6. what a complete case costs;
  7. how deterioration will be detected.

Without those answers, adding users or volume simply scales uncertainty.

Seven decisions before scaling an AI automation

1. Define a verifiable output

“Help the sales team” is too broad. “Classify an enquiry, extract five fields, prepare a source-backed summary and leave the case awaiting approval” is testable.

Write down the input, expected output and acceptance conditions. If the process itself is still unclear, begin with the guide on where AI can add value in a small business before comparing models or platforms.

2. Separate interpretation, validation and decision

AI is useful for interpreting variable text, speech and documents. A rule is better for validating a tax identifier, a spending threshold, a consent status or a date. A person should retain decisions with meaningful impact or insufficient context.

This hybrid architecture avoids asking the model to be exact where the outcome can be deterministic. The AI, rules or hybrid automation matrix helps assign the right mechanism to each step.

3. Design exceptions before volume

Real processes do not follow one happy path. There will be incomplete documents, different languages, contradictory fields, unavailable integrations and out-of-scope cases. Define a visible exception queue, an owner, a response time and an allowed outcome: correct, request information, retry or reject.

Automation is not reliable because it is often right. It is reliable when it makes failure visible and does not lose the case.

4. Restrict data, tools and permissions

A pilot may have worked with a sample folder or an administrator account. Production needs its own identity, least-privilege access, authorised sources and action logs. If the system can send messages, create records or change data, separate reading, proposing and executing.

The guide to AI agents acting across business tools explains these controls in more detail. Technical access is not the same as business authorisation.

5. Use risk-based human review

Reviewing everything removes much of the saving. Reviewing nothing turns every model error into an action. Focus review on low-confidence outputs, sensitive data, money, commitments, contradictions and periodic samples of ordinary cases.

Record what the reviewer changes. Without that feedback, the system may repeat the same mistake while the team quietly compensates for it by hand.

6. Calculate the full cost per case

Model consumption is one line, not the whole cost. Include integrations, extraction, storage, retries, human review, monitoring, incidents and maintenance. Divide the total by successful cases, not attempts.

Compare that figure with current handling time, avoidable errors and the value of a faster response. The AI automation cost-per-case calculator includes exceptions and oversight instead of presenting theoretical savings.

7. Set criteria to continue, correct or stop

Agree thresholds before the pilot begins: percentage of outputs accepted without correction, review time, cost per valid case, incidents, escalations and team satisfaction. Track serious errors separately; a strong average can conceal one unacceptable failure mode.

Scaling may mean more volume, but it can also mean enabling one extra action, adding one source or reducing review. Change one element at a time and keep a stable test set to detect regressions.

An illustrative example: qualifying incoming enquiries

Consider a business receiving enquiries through forms and email. Its pilot summarises each message and suggests a category, urgency and owner. Production does not require AI to decide which lead is “worth pursuing”. It needs a narrower, verifiable flow:

  1. the model extracts details and proposes a classification;
  2. rules check mandatory fields and prevent commitments on price or timing;
  3. low-confidence cases, complaints and contradictory data go to review;
  4. a person approves the record before any sales response;
  5. the system logs corrections, handling time and outcome.

For several weeks, the assisted process is compared with the previous one. A second phase only makes sense if it reduces time without increasing material errors and the cost per case remains sustainable. This is an illustrative example, not a client result.

What an SME should do now

The useful message from the Eurobarometer is not that a business should adopt AI because others are doing so. It is that experimentation is already common, and value appears when a trial becomes a controlled way of working.

Choose a process with enough volume, an observable outcome and manageable risk. Document data, permissions, rules, exceptions, review and cost. Train the team to use the system and to recognise when not to trust it; the guide to what businesses should document when using AI is a practical starting point.
