Does your business use AI? What you should document now

VBG Labs · 29 September 2026

If someone in your business uses ChatGPT to draft emails, Copilot to summarise documents or a tool that classifies invoices, AI is already part of day-to-day work. The question is no longer just whether the tool works. You also need to know who uses it, what data it receives, which decisions it supports and what human oversight applies.

You do not need to begin with a fifty-page manual. For an SME, a sensible starting point is much more concrete: a simple inventory, understandable rules, training suited to each role and evidence that these measures are reviewed.

The short answer: five things worth keeping

A business using AI should be able to show, at minimum:

  1. Which AI tools it uses and for what purpose.
  2. Which data may be entered and which data must not be entered.
  3. Who has received guidance or training on using those tools.
  4. Which decisions require human review before action is taken.
  5. When the tools, risks and internal rules are reviewed.

This alone does not guarantee compliance with every legal requirement. It does turn improvised AI use into something the business can govern, review and demonstrate.

Why this matters particularly now

Article 4 of the EU AI Act requires providers and deployers of AI systems to take measures that support AI literacy among people using those systems on their behalf. According to the European Commission’s official AI literacy guidance, the obligation has applied since 2 February 2025, with supervision and enforcement rules operational from August 2026.

An SME using a third-party tool will not usually be the organisation developing the model, but it may still be a deployer: it decides where the system is used, what data it receives and which business process it affects. The appropriate measures depend on the context, the people involved and the risk of that particular use.

The practical goal is not to turn every employee into a technical specialist. It is to give each person enough understanding to use the tool responsibly, spot predictable failures and recognise when to stop and request a review.

This article provides general operational guidance, not legal advice. Sensitive or regulated uses should be assessed with an appropriately qualified professional.

What AI literacy does not necessarily require

Before creating unnecessary bureaucracy, it helps to clear up four points:

  • It does not mean everybody must complete the same course.
  • It does not turn every use of ChatGPT into a complex legal project.
  • It does not mean banning AI until a perfect policy exists.
  • It is not solved by emailing everybody the tool’s instructions.

Training should relate to the work people actually perform. Someone using AI to draft emails needs guidance on personal data, verification and tone. Someone using AI to screen applicants, assess risk or support decisions with significant consequences needs much stronger controls.

1. Build a realistic inventory of AI use

Do not ask only which tools the company has purchased. Ask which tools the team actually uses, including free accounts and AI features embedded in software you already have.

For each use, record:

  • the tool and provider;
  • the responsible person or department;
  • the task it supports;
  • the type of data it receives;
  • the output it produces;
  • whether the output is reviewed;
  • the possible consequence of an error.

A spreadsheet is enough to begin. The value is not in the format. It is in finding informal uses that nobody had considered part of the company’s AI footprint.

2. Define rules people can remember

A useful policy should answer everyday questions. For example:

  • May I paste a client’s email into this tool?
  • May I upload an invoice containing a tax number, address and bank details?
  • May I send an AI-generated answer directly to a customer?
  • Who checks a calculation, recommendation or classification?
  • What should I do if the tool gives a strange answer or reveals unexpected information?

One clear page is better than a long document nobody reads. Separate permitted uses, uses requiring approval and prohibited uses. Include a simple route for asking questions.

3. Match training to the role and risk

A general session can explain what generative AI is, why it may invent information and what can go wrong when data is shared. The next step is to make the guidance specific to each team.

Administration: checking data extracted from invoices, duplicate detection and tax-related information.

Sales: verifying answers, protecting confidentiality and understanding the limits of automated customer contact.

Marketing: rights relating to source materials, transparency and human editorial review.

Management: accountability, approval of sensitive uses and criteria for buying new tools.

Keep a record of the date, the topics covered and the participants. The record does not replace learning; it shows what the business has done and helps identify who still needs guidance.

4. Write down where human review is mandatory

Saying “a person always checks it” is too vague. Define what checking means.

For an email draft, reading it before sending may be enough. For an invoice, sensitive fields should be checked against the original document. For a recommendation affecting a person, review means understanding the data used, being able to challenge the result and having genuine authority to change it.

Also define what AI may not do on its own. Approving payments, accepting contracts, rejecting candidates or giving a final answer in a sensitive complaint are examples that deserve an explicit decision.

If you are still deciding where AI or automation belongs, this guide to starting with AI in an SME helps separate useful applications from those that add risk without enough value.

5. Record incidents and review changes

Tools change, providers update their terms and teams discover new uses. A policy written once and forgotten will quickly become outdated.

Keep a simple record of:

  • incorrect outputs that had a real impact;
  • sensitive data entered by mistake;
  • automations that took an inappropriate action;
  • customer complaints or confusion;
  • significant changes to a tool or its terms.

Schedule a quarterly review of the inventory, plus an extra review whenever a new tool is introduced or an important process changes.

What about transparency for customers and users?

Article 50 of the AI Act introduces transparency obligations for certain systems and content from 2 August 2026. The Commission’s official overview of AI transparency rules covers situations such as direct interaction with an AI system, deepfakes and certain generated content on matters of public interest.

This does not mean that every internal text prepared with AI assistance needs a label. It does mean businesses should assess how a chatbot, assistant or automated piece of content is presented when someone could reasonably believe they are interacting with a human or viewing material that was not artificially generated.

The practical rule is not to design AI to deceive. If an assistant responds automatically, say so clearly and provide access to a person when that is needed.

A manageable 30-day plan

Week 1: identify tools, uses, owners and data types.

Week 2: write a one-page policy and define which decisions need supervision.

Week 3: train the team using examples from their real work and record the session.

Week 4: review the highest-risk uses, correct access controls and set the next review date.

There is no need to wait for a perfect governance structure. The important shift is from “everyone uses AI however they can” to a system in which the business knows what is happening and can improve it.

Final checklist

  • We have an inventory of AI tools and uses.
  • We know which data must not be entered.
  • Each team has received guidance suited to its work.
  • Sensitive decisions have defined human oversight.
  • Where required, we explain when a person is interacting directly with AI.
  • We record relevant incidents and changes.
  • A person and date are assigned for the next review.
