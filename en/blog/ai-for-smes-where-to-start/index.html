AI for small businesses: where to start so it actually adds value

VBG Labs · 3 June 2026

Most small businesses that call us to talk about AI have already tried something. Someone on the team uses ChatGPT to draft emails; someone else tried a chatbot and abandoned it within a week. The problem usually isn’t the tool — it’s that nobody decided what it was actually for, or how it should fit into the real day-to-day of the business.

This article is the set of criteria we use with our own clients to get started with AI without wasting time or money.

First: AI isn’t a strategy, it’s a tool

It sounds obvious, but it’s worth saying: “I want to bring AI into the business” isn’t a goal, it’s a means. Before looking at any specific tool, you need to be clear on what problem you’re actually trying to solve. The businesses that get the most out of AI aren’t the ones using the most tools — they’re the ones who applied it to two or three specific points where it genuinely adds value.

Where it tends to add real value

In our experience with small businesses and freelancers, AI tends to work well in these situations:

First response to repetitive questions. If your team answers the same questions over and over — hours, prices, availability — an AI assistant can handle the first response and only bring in a person when the question is genuinely complex.

Sorting and extracting information from documents. Invoices, emails, forms — any document that arrives in a reasonably consistent format and needs specific data pulled from it is a solid use case for AI as it stands today.

Producing a first draft. Drafting a first version of an email, a product description, or a reply to a customer is something AI does quickly and well — as long as a person reviews it before it goes out.

Finding patterns in data you already have. Which invoices are more likely to contain an error, which customers tend to pay late, which posts perform best: AI is good at spotting patterns in data that already exists, even if nobody has looked at it with that question in mind before.

Where it tends to cause problems

It’s just as important to know where AI still isn’t reliable, or where the risk isn’t worth it:

Decisions with real legal or financial consequences, without human review. An assistant can prepare the information, but the final call — approving a payment, accepting a contract — should always go through a person.

Sensitive data without clear guarantees. Before putting customer, employee or supplier data into any AI tool, you need to know exactly where that data goes and who can access it.

Fully replacing human contact in delicate moments. An upset customer, an important negotiation, a sensitive situation: AI can help prepare the response here, but it shouldn’t be the one giving it.

The most common mistakes we see

Starting with the tool, not the problem. “We want a chatbot,” said before anyone has decided what problem the chatbot is meant to solve, usually ends in an abandoned project within a few weeks.

Not training the team that has to use it. An AI tool nobody knows how to use well is worse than not having it, because it breeds distrust. That’s why we always include training whenever we implement something new.

Never talking about limits and privacy. What information is safe to put into an AI tool, and what never is, should be clear to the whole team — not assumed.

Trying to apply it everywhere at once. The projects that work usually start with one specific point, prove it works, and expand from there. The ones that try to cover everything at once often never quite ship.

Where we’d start

If we had to boil it down to one piece of advice: pick one specific, small, measurable problem, apply AI to it with a person reviewing the output, and check whether it genuinely saves time or cuts down errors before expanding to anything bigger.
