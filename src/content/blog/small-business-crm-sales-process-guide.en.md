A CRM is useful when it helps a team see which opportunity needs attention, what has been agreed and what should happen next. If it becomes little more than a contact list that nobody maintains, the tool may not be the real problem. The business often lacks a shared sales process, a minimum data set and clear ownership.

Before comparing products, define how an opportunity enters the business, which stages it can move through, what must happen before it advances and when it is considered closed. This guide sets out a small, measurable rollout that a small business can sustain without trying to build the perfect sales system on day one.

## Start with the outcome, not the feature list

“Keep every customer in one place” is a reasonable ambition, but it is hard to test. Be specific about the problem the CRM must solve during its first three months. For example:

- prevent enquiries from different channels from being lost;
- make ownership of every opportunity visible;
- show which proposals are waiting for a response;
- schedule the next follow-up instead of relying on memory;
- identify where sales tend to stall.

Choose one main outcome and one or two measures. If follow-up is the problem, track open opportunities without a next action or the number of days since the last contact. If responsiveness is the issue, measure the time between an enquiry arriving and the first useful reply.

Do not attempt to redesign marketing, sales, support, invoicing and retention at the same time. A smaller first scope makes adoption easier and helps you distinguish process problems from configuration problems.

## Map the sales process you use today

Follow two or three recent opportunities from first contact to their final outcome. Note where they came from, who handled them, what information was requested, which decisions were made and how the team knew a follow-up was due.

The [one-page process template](/en/blog/one-page-process-template-before-automation/) provides enough structure to document that journey without creating a lengthy manual. A sales process may begin with a web enquiry, phone call or referral. Its end is not always “won”: an opportunity may be lost, disqualified, deferred or waiting for information.

Keep three concepts separate:

- **Contact:** the person with whom you communicate.
- **Company or account:** the organisation they belong to, where relevant.
- **Opportunity:** a specific potential sale with a need, indicative value and next step.

One company can have several contacts and multiple opportunities over time. If everything is stored in a single record or unstructured notes, it becomes difficult to reconstruct the history and analyse the sales process later.

## Use a few stages with observable conditions

Five or six stages are usually enough for a first pipeline. Names will vary by business, but every stage should represent a genuine change:

1. **New enquiry:** it exists but has not yet been reviewed.
2. **Qualified:** there is a basic fit and the need is understood.
3. **Discovery:** the scope is being clarified through a call or assessment.
4. **Proposal sent:** the prospect has an offer or another formal next step.
5. **Decision pending:** a response, approval or agreed date is outstanding.
6. **Won or lost:** there is an outcome and its reason is recorded.

Avoid stages based only on sentiment, such as “interested” and “very interested”. Define an entry and exit condition. An opportunity should not move to “proposal sent” because someone intends to prepare it; it should move when the proposal has actually been sent and the next follow-up date has been recorded.

Do not use stages as a task list either. “Call”, “send email” and “prepare quote” are activities within a stage. Mixing status and activity makes the pipeline much less informative.

## Store only the data you will use

Every required field adds friction. If nobody can explain which decision or action depends on a piece of data, it probably should not be mandatory in the first version.

A minimum useful set might include:

- contact and company name;
- source channel and enquiry date;
- a one-sentence summary of the need;
- owner;
- current stage;
- next action and due date;
- indicative value, if it genuinely helps prioritisation or forecasting;
- reason for losing or disqualifying the opportunity.

Use controlled options for information you want to compare, such as lead source or loss reason. Keep free text for context that does not fit a classification. Avoid copying entire conversations when a short summary and a link to the original email or document will do.

Also decide who can view, edit and export the information. The ability to create dozens of fields does not mean everyone needs access to every piece of data.

## Give every opportunity an owner and a next action

An opportunity without an owner easily becomes something that “someone will check”. An opportunity without a next action can remain open for months without representing real work.

Use two simple rules:

1. every open opportunity has one accountable owner;
2. every active opportunity has a dated next action.

The action should be verifiable: “send the technical example on Tuesday”, “call after the prospect's internal meeting” or “close if there is no response after the second follow-up”. “Follow up” is too vague unless it states when and why.

Decide what happens during leave, sickness or a role change. The history should belong to the business rather than remain scattered across personal inboxes, calendars and private notes.

## Configure the minimum and test real cases

Do not migrate the entire history on day one. Start with open opportunities and the contacts the team needs now. Remove duplicates, agree naming conventions and keep an exportable copy before transforming data.

Run the CRM with a small group or a single sales route for two or three weeks. Review the following each week:

- opportunities with no owner;
- records without a next action;
- stages interpreted differently by different people;
- fields that are always left empty;
- information the team still stores elsewhere;
- reminders or tasks that create noise rather than action.

The pilot is not there to prove the product works. It is there to discover what the process needs. If the system creates duplicate work or cannot represent a common exception, adjust the scope before adding more users.

## Automate only after the process has settled

Once stages and data are being used consistently, consider small automations: create an opportunity from a form, assign it according to a rule, flag an unanswered enquiry, create a task after a proposal is sent or update a dashboard.

Keep human review for decisions such as disqualifying an opportunity, changing an important priority or sending a sensitive message. Define what happens when data is missing, a duplicate appears or an integration fails. The guide to [what is worth automating](/en/blog/what-is-worth-automating/) helps separate repeatable steps from those that still need judgement.

Before buying add-ons or building integrations, ask whether the standard CRM already solves the problem, whether a configurable layer is enough or whether the requirement is genuinely distinctive. The [off-the-shelf, no-code and custom software comparison](/en/blog/off-the-shelf-no-code-custom-software-comparison/) provides a structured way to make that choice.

## Measure sales health, not every movement

Begin with four indicators that could trigger a decision:

- new enquiries by source;
- time to first useful contact;
- open opportunities without a next action;
- win, loss and disqualification rates and reasons.

Call and email counts can provide context, but more activity does not automatically mean a better process. A dashboard should reveal where intervention is needed: a source producing poor-fit enquiries, proposals with no follow-up or a stage with an excessive wait.

Review definitions monthly during the pilot. If two people interpret a metric differently, fix the definition first. A precise chart based on inconsistent data creates confidence without clarity.

## A manageable 30-day rollout

In week one, set the objective, document the current process and agree the stages. In week two, define the minimum fields, permissions and ownership rules. In week three, configure the tool and add only open opportunities. In week four, review real cases, remove friction and choose one improvement to test next.

By the end of the month, you should be able to answer three questions: what is open, who owns it and what happens next. If you still cannot, adding more automation or reports will not solve the underlying problem.
