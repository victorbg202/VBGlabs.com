On 17 November 2026, the European Commission opened a call to identify up to 100 mature European artificial intelligence solutions that are ready for deployment in SMEs. Selected products will form the future “EDIH Ready” catalogue on the AI-on-Demand Platform. For a small business, this is not a label that removes the need for due diligence. It is a new route for finding providers and testing use cases with support from European Digital Innovation Hubs (EDIHs).

## What the Commission has actually announced

The [European Commission AI Office call](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/call-expression-interest-european-ai-providers) is for European AI providers, not SME buyers. This first edition is open to up to 100 solutions and closes at 17:00 CET on 17 December 2026. It may close earlier if the limit of suitable submissions is reached.

Proofs of concept, research-only projects and minimum viable products that are not yet market-ready are not eligible. Providers must be headquartered in an eligible country, offer a mature AI solution that addresses concrete SME needs, and complete the application in English.

Solutions considered suitable will be onboarded to the [AI-on-Demand Platform](https://www.aiodp.ai/) and identified as “EDIH Ready” in its Business Navigator. The Commission expects onboarding to begin in the second half of January, followed by sessions with EDIHs in the first quarter of 2027. As of 18 November, the catalogue is therefore not yet a complete list that an SME can buy from.

## Why this matters to an SME

Finding products that claim to use AI is rarely the hard part. Comparing them with enough context is: whether they fit the real process, work with the available data, route the right exceptions to a person and remain affordable as volumes rise.

[European Digital Innovation Hubs](https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/home) act as local and regional access points. The network offers technical expertise, training, ecosystem connections, support in accessing finance and “test before invest” services. According to the call, EDIHs have already delivered services to more than 50,000 organisations across Europe.

The future catalogue may reduce the first layer of search: European solutions with a minimum level of maturity and an SME focus. It may also help an EDIH connect a business need with providers that have already described their use case, deployment requirements and sector fit.

It will not make every listed solution equivalent or prove that any one of them is right for your business.

## What “EDIH Ready” does — and does not — mean

The Commission presents “EDIH Ready” as an identifier in a catalogue and a practical resource for EDIHs. It does not describe it as legal certification, a complete security audit or a guarantee of return. That distinction matters.

A catalogue entry may show that the provider and solution passed this initiative's filter. It does not replace:

- functional testing with documents, conversations or data that represent your process;
- assessment of privacy, permissions, hosting and data retention;
- review of the obligations that apply to your particular use of AI;
- integration testing with the business systems involved;
- a full cost calculation, including review, exceptions and maintenance;
- a contract covering service levels, responsibilities and a clear exit route.

The call also says the Commission may remove solutions that are no longer considered suitable for SME deployment. That is another reason to treat the catalogue as a living resource, not permanent approval.

## Seven questions to ask before testing an AI solution

### 1. Which business result should improve?

Define an observable output: preparing an incoming invoice for review, answering questions from approved documents or classifying enquiries with evidence. “Adding AI” is not an objective. If that is still where the discussion stands, start with [AI for small businesses: where to begin](/en/blog/ai-for-smes-where-to-start/).

### 2. Which part genuinely needs a model?

Separating interpretation, rules and actions avoids paying a model to perform deterministic checks. The [AI, rules and hybrid automation matrix](/en/blog/ai-or-rules-automation-decision-matrix/) helps assign each step to the most reliable mechanism.

### 3. What data has demonstrated that it works?

Ask for a test with representative, anonymised samples: normal cases, exceptions, poor-quality documents, conflicting instructions and missing data. A demonstration prepared by the provider is not the same as your process.

### 4. How does it expose errors and uncertainty?

A useful solution should be able to abstain, show where an answer came from and route doubtful cases for review. Ask what happens when the model, an API or the destination system fails. The [AI assistant testing checklist](/en/blog/ai-assistant-testing-checklist-before-production/) provides concrete cases for validating this behaviour.

### 5. What data does the provider receive, and why?

Document fields, attachments, logs, location, retention, subprocessors and any use for model training. Apply data minimisation and keep test data separate from production. If the answer is ambiguous, the pilot is not ready.

### 6. What is the cost per correctly completed case?

The licence is only one component. Include model consumption, integrations, human review, exceptions, support and future changes. The [AI automation cost-per-case calculator](/en/blog/ai-automation-cost-per-case-calculator/) lets you compare the current process with a pilot scenario without inventing savings.

### 7. How can you retrieve data and switch provider?

Confirm export formats, ownership of configurations and knowledge, cancellation timelines and what happens to integrations. A cheap trial can create expensive dependency if there is no exit plan.

## A prudent route from catalogue to pilot

Once the catalogue is live, use it as a starting point rather than a final decision:

1. document one process and one measurable outcome;
2. ask your nearest EDIH for guidance if you need testing capacity or sector expertise;
3. shortlist two or three options against the same requirements;
4. test them with the same cases and acceptance criteria;
5. review quality, time, cost, incidents and the team's experience;
6. expand only the workflow that clears the agreed thresholds.

A pilot needs a duration, volume, owner and stop condition. It should also state which actions still require human approval. Without those boundaries, comparison turns into a series of demonstrations that are difficult to assess.

## What AI providers should do now

A European provider with a mature SME solution can review the FAQ and submit before the deadline. It should be ready to explain the problem solved, target sectors, deployment requirements, integrations, required data, human controls and available evidence. The call is not designed for an idea, a proof of concept or an MVP that cannot yet be deployed.

An SME buyer does not need to wait passively for the catalogue. The work that most affects success happens earlier: defining the process, preparing test cases and deciding what counts as a sufficient improvement.
