When someone calls us for the first time, they almost always open with the same line: "I want to automate the business." It's a reasonable thing to say, and much too broad to act on. Automation isn't a goal — it's a tool, and like any tool, it only helps when it's pointed at the right problem.

This article is the exact criteria we use ourselves, every time, before proposing any automation. It's not theory — it's the practical filter that stops us proposing (and billing you for) something that doesn't actually suit you.

## The wrong question

"What can I automate?" is the wrong question, because almost any task can technically be automated. The right question is different: **what's worth automating, given the time it'll save, the risk it removes, and what it will cost to build?**

That means some tasks *could* be automated and we still won't recommend it — not because we can't, but because the numbers don't work out.

## Three questions before deciding anything

Before proposing any automation, we always ask the same three questions about the process in question.

### 1. How often does it happen?

A task you do five times a day is a far better candidate than one you do once a month. It's not just about total time saved — frequent tasks are also the ones where failures get noticed fastest, and the ones generating the most day-to-day friction even when each instance is quick.

Rule of thumb: if something happens more than ten times a month, it's worth a closer look. If it happens once a month or less, there are probably more urgent places to put the effort.

### 2. What's the risk if it goes wrong?

Some repetitive tasks don't matter much if they fail — you fix it and move on. Others carry real consequences: a duplicate invoice that gets paid twice, an appointment that never got written down and a customer is lost, a tax figure calculated wrong. The higher the cost of a mistake, the more it's worth investing in a system that doesn't depend on someone remembering.

### 3. What would it actually cost to build properly?

This is where a lot of automation projects fail before they start: something complex gets built to save ten minutes a week. If the cost to build it — in time, money, and future upkeep — is higher than what you'll save over a year or two, it's usually not worth it yet.

## The signal that it IS worth it

When all three answers point the same way — it happens often, mistakes are costly, and building it is relatively straightforward — that's when automation genuinely makes sense. It's exactly the pattern we see most often in small and mid-sized businesses:

- **Orders or questions that arrive on WhatsApp** and get written down by hand: frequent, with a real risk that one slips through, and technically simple to register automatically.
- **Received invoices** that someone types into a spreadsheet every month: repetitive, prone to transcription errors, and there are already good tools to solve it.
- **Appointment or payment reminders** that depend on someone remembering: they happen daily, forgetting is costly, and the fix is technically simple.

## The signal that it ISN'T worth it (yet)

Just as important is knowing when to leave something alone. Some common cases:

- **A process that isn't defined yet.** If everyone on the team does it differently, automating it first means building on an inconsistent foundation. Sort the process out before building on top of it.
- **A task that happens rarely.** If it only happens once a quarter, it's probably not even worth the time to scope it properly.
- **A problem that's solved by tidying up, not automating.** Sometimes the fix is simply deciding where information lives and who owns it — no new tool required.

## What we do when the answer is "don't touch it"

We say so directly when that's the case. It happens regularly: someone comes to us wanting to automate a process, and once we've looked closely, the best advice is to leave it alone for now, or just tidy up what's already there. It's not what a vendor selling automation would tell you. It's what we'd tell you if we were in your shoes.
