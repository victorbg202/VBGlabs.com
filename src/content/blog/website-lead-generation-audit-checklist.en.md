A website does not create opportunities simply because people visit it. A suitable visitor needs to understand what you offer, who it is for, what to do next and what will happen after that. This audit reviews the whole path in about 30 minutes and produces a score from 0 to 50, without treating more traffic as proof of a better sales journey.

It is not a full SEO, legal or accessibility audit. It is a practical way to find friction between someone landing on the website and your team following up their enquiry. Start with one important page: the homepage, a service page or a specific campaign landing page.

## How to score the checklist

Give every criterion **0, 1 or 2 points**:

- **0:** it is missing or cannot be verified;
- **1:** it exists but is ambiguous, incomplete or inconsistent;
- **2:** it is clear, works and has an owner who can maintain it.

There are five sections with five criteria each, giving a maximum of 50. Do not score whether you personally like the design. Check whether the page helps a particular person make the next decision.

Before opening the page, complete two lines:

```text
PRIORITY VISITOR: Who needs to understand this page?
PRIMARY ACTION: What should they do after reading it?
```

If your team cannot agree on the answers, you have already found the first task. One page does not need to solve every problem for every audience.

## Section 1. A clear proposition — 10 points

- [ ] **Recognisable audience.** The right visitor can tell that the service is intended for a business like theirs.
- [ ] **Specific problem.** The opening explains the problem being addressed, not just a broad label such as “digital solutions”.
- [ ] **Understandable outcome.** It is clear what could improve when there is a fit, without absolute promises or invented figures.
- [ ] **Scope and limits.** The page distinguishes what is included, what is not and when another solution may be needed.
- [ ] **Easy to scan.** Headings, paragraphs and lists make the essential message clear on a mobile screen.

Run a simple test. Show only the opening section for a few seconds to someone unfamiliar with the project. Ask what the business offers and what they would do next. Do not explain the answer; record the words they understood.

Avoid opening with a long company history when the visitor does not yet know why it matters. Identity and experience build trust, but the problem needs to be placed first.

## Section 2. Trust without unnecessary friction — 10 points

- [ ] **Visible identity.** The business name, contact route and basic information are easy to find.
- [ ] **Realistic expectations.** The process, requirements or working method are explained without guaranteed outcomes.
- [ ] **Honest evidence.** Cases, reviews, credentials and figures appear only when they can be substantiated.
- [ ] **Privacy and consent.** Forms, analytics and cookies reflect how information is actually processed.
- [ ] **No obvious access barriers.** Content can be read, enlarged and navigated with a keyboard; important images have alternatives and controls are identifiable.

This section is not a substitute for a legal or accessibility review. It exposes visible inconsistencies: a form collecting information nobody needs, a generic “Submit” button that does not explain the action or a sales statement the business cannot support.

Try the journey on a small screen and a slow connection too. If an animation, banner or image prevents the main content from appearing, it is not merely a visual preference. It is blocking the decision.

## Section 3. Call to action and form — 10 points

- [ ] **One primary action.** The page prioritises one decision: make contact, request a review, book or download a resource.
- [ ] **Specific wording.** The button describes the action —such as “Tell us about your case”— rather than using a detached “Learn more”.
- [ ] **Appropriate placement.** The call appears after enough context and returns near the end without competing with five alternatives.
- [ ] **Proportionate form.** Only the information needed to respond to or qualify the enquiry is requested.
- [ ] **Confirmation and fallback.** The visitor knows whether submission worked, what happens next and how to make contact if it fails.

Not every visitor should be sent straight to a meeting. Choose the next step that fits the page and the service.

Test the form end to end. Use test data, deliberately enter an invalid field and inspect the success message. Check who receives the notification and where the enquiry is recorded. An attractive form that sends messages to an unattended inbox is not a lead system.

## Section 4. Measurement that supports decisions — 10 points

- [ ] **Defined conversion.** The team agrees which action counts as an opportunity and which is only an interaction.
- [ ] **Preserved source.** Campaigns and links keep a useful source value instead of overwriting it at every step.
- [ ] **Verified events.** Important clicks, successful submissions and confirmations are recorded once with stable names.
- [ ] **Respected consent.** Measurement that needs consent does not start before the decision, and rejection works too.
- [ ] **Actionable review.** A named owner reviews the journey with a specific question, not just a dashboard full of charts.

Capturing every possible interaction does not necessarily produce better judgment. Start with page visits, form starts, successful submissions and enquiry source. If a data point changes no decision, it can wait.

## Section 5. Follow-up after the click — 10 points

- [ ] **One owner.** Every new enquiry reaches an assigned person or clearly managed queue.
- [ ] **Next action.** Response, qualification and disqualification have a criterion and date.
- [ ] **Preserved context.** The team can see the page, service, need and consent attached to the enquiry.
- [ ] **Duplicates and absence covered.** The process anticipates repeat enquiries, leave, integration failures and undelivered alerts.
- [ ] **Regular test.** Someone submits a test enquiry and checks the website, email, CRM, response and closure.

Document this path with the [one-page process template](/en/blog/one-page-process-template-before-automation/). It makes the boundary between the website and human work visible. Automating an alert may help; automating an unsuitable response merely makes the mistake faster.

## Interpret the score without chasing perfection

| Score | Recommended priority |
|---|---|
| **0–20** | Clarify the audience, proposition, primary action and receipt of enquiries. |
| **21–35** | Remove friction and test the complete form journey. |
| **36–44** | Improve measurement, attribution and follow-up before buying more traffic. |
| **45–50** | Maintain controls and test small changes with evidence. |

The score orders work; it does not predict sales. Two websites with the same result can serve very different markets, offers and sales cycles.

## An illustrative example

Imagine a technology consultancy page that lists many services but never states which problem it solves first. It has three competing buttons, requests eight fields and sends every response to a shared inbox with no owner. Its initial score might be 24 out of 50.

The team decides to focus on small businesses that need to organise a process before choosing a tool. It rewrites the opening, keeps one call to action, shortens the form, records the service and source, assigns an owner and tests the entire path. A repeat audit might score 41. This illustrates the method; it is not a real client case or a promise of more enquiries.

## Turn the checklist into a short plan

Do not try to repair all 25 items at once. Select:

1. one **0** that prevents people understanding the proposition;
2. one **0** that could cause an enquiry to be lost;
3. one **1** that can be tested this week.

For each change, record the owner, test date and evidence that will show it works. Repeat the audit after implementation.
