For a small business, the best solution is not necessarily the most customised one. Off-the-shelf software is usually the best starting point for a common process. No-code or low-code fits when existing tools need to be connected or adapted without building an entire product. Custom development becomes credible when the process is distinctive, stable and valuable enough to justify ongoing maintenance.

A sound decision compares total cost, process fit, integrations, data control and the ability to change. This matrix helps you do that before requesting proposals.

## The three options, without the sales labels

**Off-the-shelf software** is a product already built to solve a shared need, such as accounting, CRM, bookings, project management or customer support. It is commonly sold as a subscription, with the provider maintaining the application.

**No-code or low-code** uses existing components to configure workflows, forms, databases and automations. “No-code” does not mean no technical work. Someone still has to design the process, set permissions, handle failures and maintain connections. The difference is that less application code is written specifically for the business.

**Custom software** is developed for the organisation. It may be a complete application or just the missing layer between existing systems. It provides more control, but updates, security and continuity become responsibilities that someone must own.

These are not mutually exclusive choices. A good solution might combine a standard CRM, configured automations and a small custom component for the process that genuinely differentiates the business.

## A quick comparison

| Criterion | Off-the-shelf software | No-code / low-code | Custom development |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time to launch | Fast when the process fits | Medium; configuration and testing required | Slower; discovery and development required |
| Initial cost | Low or moderate | Moderate | High |
| Ongoing cost | Subscriptions based on users or usage | Subscriptions, operations and maintenance | Infrastructure, support and evolution |
| Flexibility | Limited to product options | Good for bounded workflows | High within the scope built |
| Integrations | Those offered by the vendor | Many connectors, with limits | Can be built where APIs or viable access exist |
| Data control | Depends on contract and export options | Distributed across several platforms | Can be high, but must be designed |
| Dependency | Main software vendor | Multiple tools and connectors | Team, documentation and technology stack |
| Internal maintenance | Low | Medium | High |

The table does not produce an automatic winner. A low entry price can become expensive with thirty users, multiple add-ons or significant manual work left over. A custom application can remain useful for years or become difficult to support if it lacks an owner, tests and documentation.

## A five-part decision matrix

Score each criterion from **0 to 3**. The aim is not mathematical precision. It is to make the team explain why it needs one approach rather than another.

### 1. Process fit

- **0:** this is a common process and we can adapt to an established tool.
- **1:** we need a few specific fields, rules or reports.
- **2:** the workflow has several exceptions that create genuine value.
- **3:** the process differentiates the business, and a generic tool would remove that advantage.

### 2. Required integrations

- **0:** the tool can operate independently.
- **1:** simple file imports or exports are enough.
- **2:** it must synchronise with two or three systems through APIs.
- **3:** it coordinates several systems in real time, and failures have an operational impact.

### 3. Data, permissions and traceability

- **0:** low-sensitivity data and simple permissions.
- **1:** basic roles and a change history.
- **2:** strict separation between customers, locations or teams.
- **3:** specific audit, residency, retention or access-control requirements.

A high score does not automatically justify custom development. First check whether a specialist product already meets those requirements better than a new project would.

### 4. Rate of change

- **0:** the process still changes every week.
- **1:** the objective is known, but many rules are not.
- **2:** the process is stable and exceptions are documented.
- **3:** there is meaningful volume, a process owner and data for validating the result.

This score works differently: a low result favours a configurable pilot, not a large build. Developing around an unstable process turns each new lesson into an expensive change.

### 5. Financial and strategic impact

- **0:** the process is infrequent and the problem has little cost.
- **1:** it saves time but does not constrain the business.
- **2:** it affects several users or customers every day.
- **3:** it limits sales, capacity, quality or a differentiated service.

Use the [manual task cost calculator](/en/blog/manual-task-cost-automation-calculator/) to estimate this part with evidence. Include time, errors, supervision and maintenance, not only the licence fee.

## How to read the result

Add the five scores, for a maximum of 15:

- **0–4: start with off-the-shelf software.** Configure the minimum, test the actual workflow and avoid customisation until a measurable limitation appears.
- **5–9: explore a configured or hybrid solution.** A standard tool plus automation or a no-code layer may close the gap without taking ownership of a full product.
- **10–15: assess custom development.** This is not an instruction to buy. It is a reason to complete functional discovery, estimate total cost and compare the result with specialist products.

Three conditions can override the score. If the process has no owner, nobody can define a correct result, or there is no maintenance budget, it is not ready for a custom build. ## Worked example: handling customer requests

Consider a company receiving enquiries by web form, email and phone. Two people classify them, request missing information and create tasks in an internal system.

If the need is simply to centralise requests, assign owners and track status, a standard ticketing system or CRM is likely to win. If the company already has those tools but needs to connect the form, notify the team and create tasks, no-code or low-code automation may be enough.

Custom development becomes more plausible when classification controls a proprietary workflow, customers have complex rules, data must be separated between organisations and the volume justifies a dedicated interface. Even then, retaining the CRM and building only the distinctive module may be the better design.

## Calculate total cost, not just year one

Compare the alternatives over three years and include:

- configuration, migration and data cleaning;
- licences per user, transaction or usage volume;
- integrations and add-ons;
- training and adaptation time;
- automation monitoring;
- fixes, updates and security;
- data export or supplier change;
- internal hours the new system will continue to require.

Ask for a demonstration using two real cases and one difficult exception. A prepared demo shows the ideal path. A test with your data exposes the fit, permissions, errors and manual steps that remain.

## Checklist before making the decision

Do not approve a purchase or development project until you can answer:

- what exact problem it solves and how success will be measured;
- who owns the process and the tool;
- what data enters, where it is stored and how it can be exported;
- which systems must be connected;
- which exceptions need human review;
- what happens when an integration fails;
- what it will cost over three years;
- who will maintain it;
- how it will be tested with a limited scope;
- what conditions would lead you to stop or replace it.

The best decision is usually the smallest solution that solves the problem well and creates room to learn.
